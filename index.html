The Story in Brief

On the windy edge of Cape Wren stands a lighthouse, and in the lighthouse lives Mara Quill, a keeper who writes down every ship she has ever seen in a battered brown ledger. For hundreds of pages the story is exactly that: quiet nights, the sweep of the lamp, the slow arithmetic of tides, and a winter in which no ships come at all.

Then a stranger washes up at the foot of the rocks. He carries a coded notebook, and Mara slowly realizes that the cipher hidden in the margins of her own ledger matches his. The harbor town below grows suspicious, the lamp refuses to light on the worst night of the year, and the salvage court threatens to take the tower away from her. Up to this point, roughly the first five hundred pages, the summary and the real story walk side by side.

After that, the tale turns into something else entirely: the story of Alden Voss, a soft-spoken city clerk who is talked into running for office. He rides a rented campaign bus across the province, survives a disastrous debate, and wins the governor's seat by a margin of eleven votes.

Power does not suit him gently. A scandal over a missing pension fund breaks in his second year, his closest adviser resigns on live television, and a long, bitter recount decides whether his career survives. The final pages follow Voss alone in the empty governor's office, deciding whether to cling to the chair or to tell the truth.

In one sentence: a lighthouse keeper's mystery that grows into a political drama about honesty and power. Nothing else of note happens, and there is nothing else worth reading.
